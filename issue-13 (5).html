— July 26, 2026 · Issue No. 13

The free trial is over, and the subscription renewed itself.

The free trial ends, and here is the weird part. Nothing happens. There is no alarm, no final warning in bold. Then one morning you wake up to your bank statement, and there it is. The charge, sitting right there.

You might be wondering what the "subscription" actually is. It’s the tariffs that were due to expire this week and didn’t. The old tariffs ran on Section 122, capped at 150 days. Section 301, the authority behind the new ones, was written to run four years, then renew on request, indefinitely, with no cap on the rate. The bill didn’t lapse. It converted to a plan that keeps going up.

✦ The signal

The real news isn’t the size of the tariff, it’s what it’s built on. Section 122 stops dead after 150 days unless Congress acts. Section 301 doesn’t stop on its own either — and this is a floor, not a ceiling, with another investigation already underway.

✦ The noise

The day-one price shock is noise. Rates barely moved, up a point or two from 11.8%. The forced-labor framing explains the legal route; the cost structure underneath is what actually impacts markets and you.

What’s moving in markets

Import-heavy shares carry the pressure this week. Now that the tariff renews rather than expires, the trade-off between smaller profits and higher prices stops being a one-off decision and becomes a permanent condition of doing business — hitting consumer discretionary names hardest, since shoppers can simply wait on a purchase they don’t need yet.

Bond yields face a mild upward tilt, since tariffs are inflationary and central banks have less room to cut into sticky prices. The dollar is a mixed story: higher-for-longer rates support it, but an unpredictable policy pulls the other way. Energy was deliberately exempt, to avoid a single input rippling through the cost of almost everything else.

Why this matters to you

If you’re building financial literacy: when a cost like this takes effect, ask two questions in sequence — who actually pays it, and does it expire or does it renew? That turns a vague, alarming headline into something you genuinely understand.

If you’re an investor: you may be more tariff-exposed than you realize, depending on how import-reliant the companies you own are. Retail and electronics carry it directly; domestic and services businesses barely notice it.

“Will you know to ask who’s actually paying it?”

It’s rarely the country that gets named in the headline. It’s an importer, absorbing the cost or passing it on. That single question is the one that turns a policy headline into something you actually understand.
